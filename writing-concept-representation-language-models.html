Critical Analysis and Reflection of Concept Representation in Language Models

This short literature review asks how researchers can study concept representation in large language models when the evidence is indirect and depends on the method being used. I compared five approaches and looked at their assumptions, strengths, and limitations.

About the paper

The paper asks what it means for a language model to represent a concept and how this can be tested. Different methods define and measure concepts in different ways, so their results cannot be compared fairly without considering those assumptions.

Methods reviewed

COPEN-style probing

Task-based benchmarks test whether a model can use conceptual distinctions in controlled prompts and evaluation tasks.

Geometric analysis

This approach studies whether categories and hierarchies appear as meaningful structures in the model’s representation space.

Semantic-relation evaluation

Prompt-based tests compare a model’s knowledge of semantic relations with human judgements.

Concept bottleneck models

Architectural interventions introduce named concepts into the model pipeline to support interpretation or control.

Potemkin-style consistency tests

These tests examine whether a model can apply a concept consistently, rather than only produce a convincing explanation of it.

Main point

No single method gives a complete answer. The studies suggest that language models capture some categorical, relational, and hierarchical patterns, but the result depends strongly on how a concept is defined and tested. A model may also explain a concept successfully without applying it consistently in a different task.

Rewritten abstract

Large language models perform well on many tasks, but it is still difficult to determine how they represent and use concepts. This paper compares five approaches: COPEN-style probing, geometric analysis of representation spaces, semantic-relation evaluation, concept bottleneck models, and Potemkin-style consistency tests. For each approach, I describe how concepts are defined, how the method evaluates them, and what its main strengths and limits are. Taken together, the studies suggest that language models learn some categorical, relational, and hierarchical structure, but they also show clear gaps compared with human understanding. The conclusions depend heavily on the assumptions and design of each evaluation method.
