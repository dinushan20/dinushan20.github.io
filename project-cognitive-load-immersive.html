Assessing Cognitive Load in Immersive Environments

This was an 8-credit group research project at Ulm University. We tested whether pupil-based measures, especially the Low/High Index of Pupillary Activity (LHIPA), could distinguish lower and higher cognitive load across desktop and VR setups. Participants completed 1-back and 3-back tasks while we recorded pupil data with different eye-tracking systems.

What I worked on
Literature review, participant sessions, data-processing pipeline, and report contribution
Collaborators
Christian Thimm and Joshua Wilson
Supervision
Tobias Wagner and Markus Sasalovici
Area
HCI, VR, cognitive load, and eye tracking
Methods
N-back task, SMEQ, pupillometry, LHIPA, IPA, and baseline-corrected pupil change
Devices
Pupil Labs Neon, Tobii Pro Spark, and Meta Quest 3 with integrated Neon

What we wanted to find out

VR can place different sensory and interaction demands on a user than a desktop setup. We wanted to know whether LHIPA could work as a stable workload measure across both environments and across different eye-tracking devices, rather than only under one recording condition.

The wider aim was to compare subjective mental effort with pupil-based measures and understand how much the recording setup and data quality affected the result.

What I did

I reviewed related work, contributed to parts of the study design, helped run participant sessions, and worked on the data-processing pipeline. My work included aligning task logs, SMEQ responses, and eye-tracking recordings; cleaning and segmenting the data; and preparing SMEQ, LHIPA, IPA, and PupilDiff values for later analysis.

Study setup

Participants completed the same 1-back and 3-back tasks in three setups:

  1. Desktop with a head-mounted Pupil Labs Neon
  2. Desktop with a stationary Tobii Pro Spark
  3. Immersive VR with a Meta Quest 3 and integrated Neon

Before the main experiment, participants received an explanation of the study, gave informed consent, and practised the N-back task. Each task block began with a 30-second baseline and continued with either the 1-back or 3-back task while gaze and pupil data were recorded. Participants completed an SMEQ mental-effort rating after each block.

The environment order was balanced as far as scheduling allowed. The 1-back and 3-back order was counterbalanced, and the task appeared in the upper-left, centre, and bottom-right positions across the experiment.

Participant flow

We recruited 18 participants. Data from 15 participants were included in the final analysis after 3 participants were excluded because of data problems.

Recording setups

The three setups allowed us to compare desktop and VR conditions while also examining differences between stationary and head-mounted eye-tracking systems.

Block structure

Each block contained a 30-second baseline, the N-back task, and an SMEQ rating. Task responses were collected by button press.

Measures

We used one subjective measure and three pupil-based measures. This allowed us to compare how difficult the task felt to participants with changes found in the pupil signal.

LHIPA was the main pupil-based measure. The Index of Pupillary Activity (IPA) and baseline-corrected pupil-diameter change (PupilDiff) were used as complementary measures.

SMEQ

Participants completed the Subjective Mental Effort Questionnaire after every task block.

LHIPA

LHIPA was the main pupil-based measure. It is calculated from changes in pupil-diameter oscillations over time and was used to compare workload across tasks and recording setups.

IPA and PupilDiff

The Index of Pupillary Activity (IPA) and baseline-corrected pupil-diameter change (PupilDiff) were used as complementary measures.

Data processing

The recordings first had to be aligned, cleaned, and divided into comparable baseline and task segments. This was especially important because the Tobii Pro Spark and Pupil Labs Neon recorded at different sampling rates.

The main processing steps were:

  1. Correct and standardise file names.
  2. Align the eye-tracking recordings with Unity and task-event timestamps.
  3. Split the recordings into baseline and task segments.
  4. Remove blinks and invalid pupil samples.
  5. Remove a 100 ms margin before and after each blink.
  6. Resample the pupil data to 60 Hz so that the Tobii and Neon recordings could be compared.
  7. Calculate LHIPA, IPA, and baseline-corrected pupil change.
  8. Combine the measures in a long-format table for analysis.

Main results

SMEQ

SMEQ ratings were higher for the 3-back task than for the 1-back task. Participants therefore experienced the 3-back condition as more mentally demanding.

LHIPA in the Neon setups

In the desktop Neon and VR Neon recordings, LHIPA decreased as task difficulty increased. The pattern was clearer in these two setups, where the pupil signal was more stable.

Tobii data

The stationary Tobii recordings contained more noise and outliers. This made the calculated LHIPA values less stable and limited direct comparison across devices.

Other measures and task position

IPA and PupilDiff gave additional views of the workload response. PupilDiff also appeared to be more sensitive to the position of the task on the display.

What the results mean

The results do not show that LHIPA is a universally reliable workload measure. It appeared promising in the cleaner Neon recordings, but its usefulness depended strongly on signal quality, blink handling, missing data, sampling rate, and task position.

The project also showed why subjective and physiological measures should be interpreted together. SMEQ gave a clear account of perceived effort, while the pupil measures showed how much the technical recording and processing choices affected the physiological result.

Limitations and next steps

Only 15 participants were included in the analysis, and the recording quality was not equally stable across all three setups. A useful next step would be to improve blink and missing-data handling and test the measures again with more consistent recording conditions.
