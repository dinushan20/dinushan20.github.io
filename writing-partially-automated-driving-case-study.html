Challenges in Partially Automated Driving and a Proposed Solution

This seminar paper looks at a problem in SAE Level 3 driving: a driver can do other things while automation is active but may still need to take control quickly. I discussed situation awareness, trust, cognitive load, and distraction, then proposed a driver-monitoring system with adaptive infotainment restrictions as one possible response.

About the paper

Partial automation changes the driver’s job. Instead of controlling the vehicle continuously, the driver may supervise the system and return to driving when a takeover request appears. This creates a difficult balance between being allowed to disengage and still being ready to respond.

This is a literature-based case study. It connects human-factors research with a possible design response; it does not report a participant study or a software implementation.

Problems discussed

Situation awareness and trust

When drivers are less involved in controlling the vehicle, they may lose track of what is happening around them. They may also trust the automation more than its actual capability supports.

Takeover and cognitive load

A takeover request can require the driver to stop another task, understand the road situation, and regain control within a short time. Rebuilding situation awareness under this pressure can create a sudden increase in cognitive load.

Divided attention and distraction

Level 3 automation can leave more attention available for non-driving activities. This makes distraction and delayed re-engagement important safety concerns.

Proposed approach

The proposed system uses driver monitoring to estimate attention and takeover readiness. Possible signals include gaze direction, facial behaviour, eye closure, and head orientation.

If the system detects reduced readiness, it could respond with an alert, a message on the driver display or infotainment screen, ambient-light changes, seat vibration, or voice feedback. Infotainment restrictions would only be applied when the situation and driver state suggested a meaningful distraction risk, rather than remaining active all the time.

Attention Signals

Estimate attention and readiness from gaze, facial behaviour, eye closure, and head orientation.

Adaptive Feedback

Use alerts, screen messages, ambient light, seat vibration, or voice feedback when the driver appears less ready to take control.

Conditional Restrictions

Limit non-driving activities only when the detected driver state and driving situation justify it.

Argument in brief

  1. Partial automation changes the driver from an active controller to a supervisor.
  2. Reduced involvement can lead to distraction, loss of situation awareness, or overtrust.
  3. A takeover request requires the driver to understand the situation and return to control quickly.
  4. Driver monitoring can estimate attention and readiness.
  5. Adaptive feedback and conditional restrictions may support safer takeover preparation.
